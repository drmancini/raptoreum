01The model

Proof of work still orders transactions and still decides the chain. Nothing about consensus changes. What changes is what a block carries.

A block records the coinbase in full and every other transaction as its 32-byte identifier. The bodies are held by the Smartnode tier and fetched back on demand. Two commitments hold it together: the merkle root, which already exists and already commits to identifiers, says what was ordered; a state root, which does not exist yet, says what the result was, so a node can begin from a verified snapshot instead of replaying history it does not have.

Nothing binds an identifier to its body except the hash itself, and nothing needs to. A body is correct exactly when it hashes to the identifier the chain already committed. That single property is what makes every fetch self-verifying and every challenge checkable by anyone.

One qualification that sections 07 and 18 develop. The state root is the mechanism, but it is not the anchor. A header chain proves work, and work is rentable. What a joining node actually rests on is a ChainLock over the snapshot base — a signature by identified, collateralised parties — which is why the Smartnode tier carries the trust here and the miners do not.

02Who holds what

Three roles, all of which this chain already has. The design gives a duty to a tier that exists rather than inventing one.

RoleHoldsProvides
Miners and poolsState, plus a recent window of bodiesOrdering and validity, under proof of work
SmartnodesEverything, foreverAvailability of history, alongside the locks and finality they already run
Ordinary nodesState, a recent window, and whatever else they chooseIndependent validation; may prune freely

Smartnodes are already identified, collateralised, addressable from the chain itself, and organised into quorums. The chain publishes their addresses as consensus state, which means the network already carries a directory of servers that can answer any request. No new registry, no assignment scheme, no advertisement.

The recent window

Its floor is not the reorg depth. Raptoreum's round-voting mechanism reads a full round of 720 previous blocks and inspects their bodies for quorum commitments, and its result gates consensus. At a two-minute interval that is roughly a day, and every other backward-looking path — the disconnect pool, database verification, crash replay — sits well inside it.

What full replication costs

At roughly 400 bytes a transaction, each Smartnode's disk grows by about 12.6 GB per year for every sustained transaction per second.

The disguised risk

Full replication couples storage cost to consensus participation. Under partial retention an operator short of disk drops data and keeps serving. Under full replication the same operator drops out entirely, taking ChainLocks signing, InstantSend and quorum participation with it.

So the failure mode of storage becoming a problem is not a degraded archive you notice and fix. It is a thinning Smartnode set. Watch throughput, which gives years of warning, not disk complaints, which give weeks.

When to act

Partial retention is a protocol change plus an incentive change plus an activation window with third parties in it. Call it a year from decision to live, with margin on top. So the trigger is less than about two years of headroom on a commodity box, which is roughly where annual growth crosses a terabyte per node. Not when disks fill.

An attacker can pull that trigger for you

The storage burden exists only because Smartnodes must retain bodies, which makes it a weapon. Filling every block costs pocket change in transaction fees while imposing hundreds of gigabytes a year of permanent storage on every Smartnode, and nothing compensates it — rewards are round-robin with no service component.

So the throughput you are watching is not only organic demand. A few thousand RTM buys the terabyte-a-year trigger outright, which moves the deferred incentive question up the list rather than leaving it to be decided by adoption.

03Two regimes

A block on the wire is the coinbase plus a list of hashes. To use it, a node needs the actual transactions. The two regimes are the two very different answers to where those come from, and which applies depends only on how old the block is.

At the tip

Consider what happened in the two minutes before the block appeared. The miner picked those transactions out of its own mempool, and they arrived there by ordinary relay, exactly as today. So by the time the block shows up, every node has already seen nearly all of them and is holding them in memory. Your node reads the list of hashes and assembles the block out of what it was already holding. That is not new machinery; it is what compact blocks do today.

Now the part that matters. The miner had to build a complete block in order to mine it, so it held every transaction, and so did every node that assembled and connected the block. Each of them keeps the recent window on disk, commitments and bodies alike, so any of them can rebuild that complete block on demand. At the tip, complete blocks genuinely exist on many machines. If assembly fails, there is a safety net: ask a peer for the whole block, and the peer can send one.

In history

Now a node reads block 800,000 off its disk a year later. Same shape, just hashes. But those transactions are in nobody's mempool. They were spent and forgotten long ago, and no node has assembled that block recently.

Here is what is easy to miss. A complete block 800,000 does not exist anywhere, and has not since the day it was mined. The miner built one in memory, once, and then it was gone. Every node that connected it built one transiently too, then filed the commitments in one place and the bodies in another. Nobody kept the assembled object. So the safety net is not merely unavailable here. It has no possible answer. All you can do is ask a Smartnode for bodies by position, a range at a time.

Why the split earns its keep

It is a security decision first. Imagine a miner that publishes a block of hashes and never gossips the transactions. Without the fallback, nobody could assemble that block, so nobody could build on it, except the miner, which has the data. It would quietly accumulate a private lead while honest miners were stuck on the parent. That attack costs nothing. Because complete blocks do exist at the tip, and any peer that connected one can hand it over, the miner is not the only possible source and the attack evaporates.

The fallback is necessary and not sufficient

"Every node that connected it can rebuild it" is circular. A node starved of bodies cannot connect the block, so cannot become a source, and the attacker chooses who connects first. Mine normally, relay the header widely because it is tiny, release bodies to a chosen subset, and everyone else holds your block as most-work-but-unconnectable.

The fallback must be joined by a relay rule: never relay a commitment block you cannot assemble yourself. Then a withheld block never propagates as a most-work header at all. See section 18.

And it confines the new complexity. Missing data in history is a normal condition, so it needs a durable state on the block, retries against different Smartnodes, and a firm rule that such a block is never marked invalid. That whole machine only has to exist for old blocks. Recent blocks run on code that already works. The boundary between the two is the recent window, whose floor is 720 blocks, roughly a day, set by the round-voting mechanism rather than by reorg depth.

04The block format

The header survives untouched, and the reason is a property this chain already had rather than a concession anyone made for this design.

The block merkle root builds its leaves from transaction identifiers and nothing else, and this chain has no segwit, so that identifier has no witness variant to reconcile. Proof of work, header relay, chain selection by work, and the existing inclusion-proof machinery all work over a commitment block with no code written.

So a commitment block is not a new object. It is the same block structure with a second serialisation: coinbase in full, every other entry a bare 32-byte identifier.

The selector cannot live in the header

Raptoreum replaced the usual version-bit scheme with its own update voting, which owns bits 0 through 28 with the top three masked, and bits 0, 1 and 2 are already assigned. There is no free bit below 29 and nothing above it that does not break the mask.

Independently, the version field sits inside the hashed header, so a format recorded there is fixed at mining time and a materialised block could never be re-serialised in full form without changing its hash.

The selector is therefore a serialisation stream flag negotiated between peers, on the pattern segwit used. The consequence is that the format is not self-describing and peers must agree before transfer.

One correction worth carrying: inclusion proofs survive, but bloom-filtered blocks do not. The identifier-set path works over commitments alone. The bloom path runs the filter over full scripts and outpoints, so serving a filtered block needs bodies for the client's whole rescan range.

One relay rule belongs with the format

Never relay a commitment block you cannot assemble yourself. A block of bare identifiers is cheap to forward, which is exactly why forwarding one you cannot connect is dangerous: it propagates as a most-work header that nobody downstream can build on. Gating relay on self-assembly restores the atomicity that whole-block relay had for free. Section 18 explains what it closes.

05Block acceptance

This is where the design lives, and where the cost is concentrated. Everything below block connection is free. Everything above it has to learn a state that did not exist.

Below connection, nothing changes

The connect and disconnect graphs were traced in full. The special-transaction processing, the quorum block processor, the deterministic list builder, the coinbase root checks, the transaction loop, the InstantSend conflict pass, the block value and payee checks, the undo and index writes, and the entire assets subsystem all take a block by reference, none reads from disk, and none depends on byte offsets. Hand them a populated block and they cannot tell where it came from.

Above it, the boundary is acceptance, not deserialisation

Acceptance today validates bodies, persists the block, and marks it a chain candidate, all before any connection happens. In the chain selector, the have-data flag simply means connectable. So a body-incomplete block either gets that flag, becomes a candidate, is selected, and shuts the node down when connection reads it back; or it is held out of the flag, and roughly thirty call sites above connection must learn a new state.

Those sites are the ones that matter most: chain selection, the download scheduler, candidate maintenance, the consistency assertions, pruning, database verification and crash replay. There is no way to build this beside the existing path and swap it in.

Three specifics

Bit 256, not 128. Bit 128 is already the chainlock-conflict flag, used at fourteen sites and persisted, so reusing it would misread existing databases.

A new validity rung. The transactions-valid level is defined as covering per-transaction checks, signature operation counts, byte size and the merkle root. Over commitments only the root, duplicates and count are checkable, so that level cannot honestly be granted at acceptance time.

Materialisation must produce a fresh object. The block check caches its own result. If a commitment-mode check marks an object that is later filled in place, the check inside connection short-circuits and the per-transaction and signature-operation checks never run on the connect path.

The give-up rule, and its bound

A node must never mark a body-incomplete block invalid. Doing so would permanently reject a chain other nodes accept. Holding it and retrying costs only liveness, so the timeout governs retry cadence and never validity, and it stays local policy rather than becoming a consensus parameter.

Never invalid does not mean retry forever

Taken literally the rule is an attack. Mine a block committing to bodies that exist nowhere, which nothing prevents, and every node polls every Smartnode for them indefinitely. Repeat and the aggregate load grows with each block you buy.

Validity and retry are separate questions. The block stays valid-but-unconnectable forever, which is correct and costs a cheap index entry. Active fetching is bounded by per-block exponential backoff and a cap on how many incomplete blocks are chased at once. All local policy, which the rule above already permits.

The bound must not key off accumulated work. An earlier draft said to pursue only blocks with more work than the current tip. That is useless against the attacker who matters most, because a large pool mining nothing but unresolvable commitments always has more work than the honest tip. Work is precisely what such an attacker has in abundance.

Why this holds at 51%

Take the worst case: a majority pool mines nothing but blocks committing to identifiers with no transaction behind them, and never supplies data because none exists.

Unconnectable work is inert. Chain selection does not follow the heaviest chain, it follows the heaviest chain it can connect. A block whose data nobody has never becomes a candidate, so no amount of work stacked on it moves anyone's tip. That is not a property to add; it is how the code already behaves, and it was verified rather than assumed.

So honest miners never adopt the block, never pause, and keep building on the parent, because mining always extends the connected tip. The attacker's chain grows into a pile nobody follows. And it earns nothing, because its blocks are in no one's chain, so its coinbase outputs are worthless. It pays the full cost of majority hashrate and collects zero reward, while the honest chain keeps paying its own miners.

Two precisions

"Orphaned" is the wrong word, and the difference matters. A stale block lost a race: nodes hold it complete and simply prefer another. These blocks never entered the race, and because nothing may mark them invalid they are not discarded the way a stale block is. That accumulation is the real cost, and it is what the backoff bound exists to contain.

The network slows, it does not stop. The honest chain continues on the remaining hashrate, and since this chain retargets difficulty every block the interval recovers within a handful of blocks. Compare with the same hashrate spent on ordinary reorgs, which earns rewards and enables double-spends. This strategy is strictly worse for the attacker.

06The body store

Flat files in block order, not a key-value store, and addressed by position rather than by hash. Both choices follow from the same measurement.

Not a key-value store

This tree already reached that conclusion once: block data lives in flat files rather than the embedded database, because log-structured merge compaction rewrites large values repeatedly as they migrate between levels. At a terabyte a year of immutable blobs that write amplification is the dominant cost, and it buys nothing, because the data never changes.

So: bodies appended to a third file series beside blocks and undo data, with a small index mapping 32-byte keys to positions, where the values are small enough that merge behaviour is harmless. This is the existing pattern, and it is what the literature calls key-value separation. RocksDB's blob mode is the packaged version, at the cost of a large dependency and the property below.

Assembly locality decides it

Bodies are written in block order, so assembling a block is one contiguous read. Assembly is the hot path: every block, every node, on the critical path to connecting the tip. A key-ordered store scatters one block's bodies across the entire dataset and turns that single read into thousands of random ones. The identifier is a hash, so key order is random order.

Addressed by position

Commitment blocks are the chain, so every node holds every commitment forever, which makes a height and an index a universal address in a way it never was before. That gives four things at once: it is more compact than a 32-byte hash, it is self-verifying because the body must hash to the identifier already committed at that position, it lets the server read contiguously, and it demotes the global identifier index from mandatory to optional. At a hundred transactions a second that index would be roughly 200 GB a year of pure random-access structure wanting to live in memory and failing to.

A serving node then needs only a per-block offset table. Bare-hash lookup stays useful for answering "show me this transaction", served by nodes that choose to keep the identifier index, which is precisely what that index is today and precisely as optional.

Two things to get right at the start

The write must happen during acceptance, transactionally with the commitment write, and not when transactions leave the mempool — that runs after connection has already succeeded, side-chain blocks never reach it at all, and blocks are announced to peers before it fires. And the format must be able to delete records later even though it never will at first, because retrofitting deletion into an append-only store is a data migration across every Smartnode rather than a code change.

Two corrections from the security review

Never write to the permanent store at mempool acceptance. It is tempting, because it makes assembly almost free. But unmined transactions pay no fee, so an attacker fills every node's disk at line rate for nothing until the node aborts on low disk — which on a Smartnode is a ban two quorum rounds later. The mempool cap bounds memory, not the file. A cache is fine if it evicts with the mempool.

Deletion is not only future-proofing. Every losing sibling block is persisted by every node, so on an append-only store those bodies are permanent. A five percent miner publishing late blocks adds roughly 26 GB a year to every Smartnode forever. The store must actually drop the bodies of blocks that lost, once buried.

07The state root

Thirty-two bytes in the coinbase that stand in for gigabytes on disk, and the single thing that gives decoupling anybody to be useful to.

Two different things a chain holds

History is the ordered list of transactions: immutable, append-only, growing forever. State is the current situation, who owns what right now. State is small, it changes every block, and it is a pure function of history. Replay every block from genesis and you get it, deterministically.

StoreContentsCommitted today?
CoinsEvery unspent outputNo
Asset stateBalances per address per asset, and asset metadataNo
Smartnode stateDeterministic list and quorum stateYes, two merkle roots already

The existing block merkle root will not do this job. It commits to what was ordered. The state root commits to where that left things. Getting from the first to the second means replaying every transaction, which is precisely the work a node without history cannot perform.

Why it becomes necessary the moment there are tiers

Today a node syncs by downloading every block and replaying it. It computes state itself and therefore trusts nobody, which is what being a full node means. A miner holding only a recent window cannot do that: the bodies are not on its disk, and fetching all of them would mean holding everything, which is the thing the tier exists to avoid.

So it must obtain state from somebody else, and without a commitment that means trusting them. A hostile peer could hand over a coins set with an extra million RTM at an address of its choosing, and the receiving node would have no way to tell. The state root moves that trust onto proof of work instead. Fetch a snapshot from anyone, compute its fingerprint, compare against the root committed in a block that cost real work to produce. A match means it is exactly what the network agreed, and where the file came from stops mattering.

How this differs from Bitcoin's version

Bitcoin's equivalent compares a snapshot against a hash hardcoded in the software by developers, so the trust sits with them. Putting the root in the chain moves it to the miners, which is what you already trust for everything else. Kaspa does it this way, and it is the only proof-of-work chain running the pieces this design needs.

What it actually is

A hash over a set, where the set is every state element: each unspent output, each asset record. The obvious construction sorts everything and hashes it in order, which is exactly what this tree's existing set hash does, and it reads the entire database. Minutes of work, so it cannot happen every two minutes.

What is needed instead is a hash that updates incrementally. When a block spends 500 outputs and creates 600, subtract 500 elements and add 600, in microseconds, without touching the rest. That is a multiset hash: every element maps to a large number, elements combine by multiplication modulo a large prime, multiplication commutes so order does not matter, which is correct because a set has no order, and division undoes it so removal costs the same as addition. Roughly 32 bytes per block, and a few arithmetic operations per output created or spent.

From element to number

element bytes            71 B    the outpoint and coin, serialised
  --SHA256-->            32 B    a digest, and only a seed
  --ChaCha20-->         384 B    keystream
  --read as integer--> 3072 bits  the number that is multiplied

The digest is not what gets multiplied. It is expanded first, and the expansion is the point. The construction is secure only if nobody can find two different sets of elements whose products are equal. Multiplying 256-bit values would leave an attacker room to hunt for multiplicative relations, choosing elements whose numbers share convenient factors and combining them into a collision. A 3072-bit group takes that away. The modulus has the form two-to-the-n minus a small constant, which makes reduction a shift and a small multiply-add rather than a division.

Three things the trace makes concrete. Additions and removals cost the same, because a spend multiplies into the denominator rather than dividing the numerator, so nothing on the per-element path needs an inverse. Disconnecting is the same trace backwards, swapping which accumulator each element joins, with no rebuild. And the size of the set appears nowhere; only how busy the block was.

What it costs

The comparison that settles it is against work the node already does. Every input in a block requires a signature verification, and that same input triggers exactly one accumulator multiplication.

Per inputCost
Signature verification~60–100 µs
Accumulator update~2–4 µs

So the accumulator adds a few percent to cryptography already on that path. At a hundred transactions a second the per-block total stays under a tenth of a second, against a two-minute interval. It stops being free at the headline figure of 2,600 a second, where it becomes a second or two inside block connection with the main lock held. Multiplication commutes and associates, so the elements split across cores and the partial products recombine, but that has to be done deliberately rather than assumed. Every figure here is an estimate from the structure of the operation and wants measuring before anything depends on it.

The root is not an anchor on its own

A header chain proves work, not validity, and work is rentable by the hour. A node that cannot replay therefore accepts any state whose root sits in a most-work chain, which is SPV security where a replaying full node today cannot be fooled by any amount of hashrate. An earlier draft called moving trust to the miners the win. It is the win for availability and the loss for validity.

Solana has run validators off snapshots for years and does not solve this cryptographically either. It stacks three partial answers, and the load-bearing one is not the hash.

The middle leg cannot be proof of work, and that is the whole point. Work behind a header is rentable by the hour; collateral behind a signature is not.

  2. 02

The honest statement

Quorum membership derives from a chain that bottoms out at a shipped value, exactly as Solana's does. Nobody escapes an anchor. The question is only how thin it is and how many independent checks stack above it, and an identified set is a better place to rest it than rented hashrate.

Note this is what the attestation decision in section 11 buys a second time. The attributable attestations chosen for retention accountability are the same signal this anchor needs.

The field is 32 bytes; the object is not

The numerator and denominator are themselves 3072-bit numbers, so the live accumulator is 768 bytes of state, not 32. Only the finalised root is small. And that state has the same standing as the coins set itself: it cannot be recomputed on demand, because recomputation is the full scan this whole design exists to avoid.

So it has to be persisted rather than derived at startup, updated on every connect and every disconnect, and carried inside the snapshot, since a node starting from one needs the accumulator's value in order to keep updating it. The sharp edge is the flush. It must commit atomically with the coins cache. If a crash lands between the two writes, the accumulator describes one height and the coins database another, the node computes a wrong root, and it forks.

The element must be the whole coin

This chain's coin record is not Bitcoin's. It carries a type field and an extra payload, which is where time-locked transaction data lives, and the spend path enforces the lock by reading exactly those two fields. An element that omits them lets a snapshot source strip a lock so a locked coin spends early, or add one so a victim's coin becomes permanently unspendable — both with a matching root.

So the rule is: the element is the outpoint plus the coin serialised in full, and anything the spend path can read from a coin must be inside it.

Asset state is smaller than it looks, and the hard part is elsewhere

Per-address balances must never be committed. They are maintained only under an index that defaults off and clamps negatives to zero, so committing them would fork nodes by configuration. Asset ownership already lives in the unspent output set as asset scripts, with per-asset conservation enforced on every transfer, so those balances are already inside the coins multiset.

What must be committed is the asset records themselves, in full — thousands, not millions. Omit any field and a snapshot source rewrites it with a matching root: drop the owner address and the attacker becomes the owner whose signature the mint check then verifies.

The genuinely hard part is canonicality rather than volume. Identical state must produce identical bytes on every node, or roots diverge and the chain forks.

08Key processes

Six paths through the system. Only the third and fourth are genuinely new; the rest are existing paths with one step added or removed.

Producing a block

  1. 01

    Select and validateThe miner picks transactions from its own mempool and validates them against the UTXO set. It already holds every body it is about to commit to.

  2. 02

    Build the coinbaseSubsidy plus fees, the two existing merkle roots, and the new state root asserting what the chain state becomes.

  3. 03

    Emit commitmentsThe block is serialised with the coinbase in full and everything else as identifiers. The block header is byte-identical to what it would have been.

  4. 04

    PublishBodies were already gossiped as ordinary transactions before the block existed, which is what makes a block of pure identifiers propagate instantly.

Holds, hashes, publishes

Three different things, and only the third one changes.

HoldsEverything. To select transactions the miner must validate them, to build the coinbase it must total the fees, and to obtain identifiers at all it hashes the bodies. All of it from its own mempool.
HashesThe 80-byte header and nothing else. Transactions were never part of what proof of work covers, and the merkle root already commits to identifiers today. The header is byte-identical between the two designs.
PublishesHeader, coinbase in full, identifiers. The bodies are already on the network, gossiped as ordinary transactions before the block existed.

Mining hardware is untouched. A rig never sees transaction data today either. Stratum hands it two halves of a coinbase with an extranonce between them plus a merkle branch to walk, and the rig assembles, hashes and derives the root itself. Extra fields in the coinbase payload are invisible to that arrangement, which is why the work sits entirely in what a pool does after a share meets the target.

Nothing prevents a miner committing to identifiers whose bodies it does not hold. The block is then unconnectable by anyone, its producer included, so it can never be built on and is orphaned. That is the enforcement: not a prohibition, just a block that does not pay.

The transaction the miner created itself

The case that matters is a transaction that only exists at assembly time, such as a pool fee payout sized to this block's fees. Nobody has relayed it, so nobody can assemble the block.

The common case is already covered. Pool payouts normally live in the coinbase, which a commitment block carries in full by construction. This chain's coinbase already pays miner, Smartnode and founder, so coinbase-based payout needs nothing new.

Where it bites, it is a new obligation on miners

Today a pool can build a transaction during assembly and ship it inside the block, so propagation is irrelevant. Under decoupling it must relay first and wait. Broadcasting and then mining two hundred milliseconds later is not enough: most of the network will not have it, the block will not assemble, and under the relay rule it will not even spread. The pool orphans its own block through haste.

Nothing in the protocol teaches this, and pool software has never had to know it.

The escape valve already exists in the codebase. Compact blocks carry a prefilled list of transactions sent whole rather than as identifiers; today only the coinbase is prefilled, with a note in the source that the mempool could fill more. A commitment block can do the same, carrying a small number of transactions in full alongside the identifiers.

It would have to be bounded by an explicit byte budget counted against the block limit, because unbounded prefill reopens unbounded block size, which is the thing this design exists to close.

The cleaner statement: publishing no longer discharges the obligation

An earlier draft here said a pool must relay and then wait for propagation, or it orphans its own block. That was too strong. The miner holds the body and can serve it. Its peers request it, assemble, and forward, and the next hop does the same, so body and block propagate together with an extra round trip at the frontier. The cost is latency, not failure.

Orphaning happens only if the miner will not or cannot serve — it publishes commitments and goes offline, or refuses. So relay-and-wait is not required. Serving is.

Which is the better way to put the whole change. Today, publishing a block discharges the miner's obligation completely. Under this design it does not: publishing commitments is half of it, and the miner stays on the hook for any body nobody else has, until it has spread. That also makes bounded prefill a latency optimisation rather than a necessity, and it is left open on those terms.

Where the new commitments live

The coinbase reaches the header only as a hash. It is the first transaction in the block, so its hash is the first merkle leaf and the header carries only the root. That is why the header stays 80 bytes however much the coinbase grows, and why this lineage already put the Smartnode and quorum roots there rather than inventing header fields.

It is still fully committed, since changing one byte changes the coinbase hash, the merkle root, the header, and voids the proof of work. And a light client can still read it with one extra step: the coinbase transaction plus a merkle path proving it is leaf zero. That machinery already exists here, because the simplified Smartnode list diff ships exactly that pairing so a client can verify the list root against a header it trusts. The state root verifies the same way through the same code.

Accepting a block

This is where the design lives. Today a node either has a block or does not. Here, having the commitments and having the bodies become two separate facts, and a block can sit between them indefinitely.

Fetching bodies

Requests name a height and an index, not a hash. Commitment blocks are the chain, so every node holds every commitment forever, which makes a position a universal address in a way it never was before.

Reorganising

Disconnecting a block needs the full bodies, not just undo data. The disconnect path walks every transaction's inputs and outputs to unwind the address, spent and future indexes, dispatches on special-transaction type, and pushes the bodies back into the mempool. So the recent window is one window rather than two, and it can never be body-free at any depth a node might disconnect.

Following a branch never seen before is the genuinely new failure. The node holds that branch's commitments and lacks its bodies, and must fetch them from peers who may be committed to the other side. The rule is to stay on the chain you can validate, and accept sitting behind until the data arrives.

Bootstrapping a node

This is the most security-sensitive path in the design, because a joining node has no history, no state, and must ask strangers for everything. The anchor rules from section 07 are what make it safe, and they are load-bearing at every step below.

  1. 01

    Pick a base block, and require it to be ChainLockedNot merely buried. A ChainLock is a signature by an identified, collateralised quorum, and that is the anchor — not accumulated work, which is rentable by the hour. The base must also sit at least 720 blocks back, matching the window floor.

  2. 02

    Verify the lock against a quorum you derived yourselfMembership comes from the Smartnode root already committed in every coinbase, syncable from any peer and checked against headers you hold. Never from whoever is offering you the snapshot.

  3. 03

    Bound the transfer before starting itRead the record count and byte size committed beside the root, so a hostile source cannot stream hundreds of gigabytes before a mismatch becomes detectable. Fetch in chunks with per-chunk hashes so a bad source is caught early rather than at the end.

  4. 04

    Recompute, do not trustRun the accumulator over everything loaded — coins, asset records, Smartnode state — and confirm it reproduces the committed root. One pass. This proves the snapshot is the state the quorum signed, and makes the source irrelevant.

  5. 05

    Fill the window and validate forwardFetch bodies for the last 720 blocks and connect them normally from the verified base.

  6. 06

    RunA miner or ordinary node is now fully validating and holds no history at all.

Why this is a prerequisite, not a refinement

Without a verifiable snapshot, a node holding only a recent window has no route to a validated tip except replaying from genesis, which needs every body. It would then hold everything, and only Smartnodes would benefit from decoupling — but Smartnodes hold everything anyway. The state commitment is what gives the design anyone to be useful to.

What goes wrong without steps 01 and 02

Verifying only that the snapshot reproduces a root in a most-work header chain is SPV security. A header chain proves work, not validity, so a node that cannot replay will accept a state containing money that never existed if the attacker's fork looks heaviest. Today no amount of hashrate can do that to a replaying node.

Recovery is worse than the attack. To leave a bad chain the victim must disconnect below its snapshot base, where it has no bodies and no undo data, so it wedges and has to be rebuilt by hand. Step 01 is what removes this, by resting the anchor on collateral instead.

Two failure modes that are not attacks on the state

Hang without disconnect. Block download has stall detection and timeouts; the snapshot transfer and the body scheduler have none. A source that dribbles forever is currently indistinguishable from a slow one. Both need the rules blocks already have.

The address list you cannot use yet. The chain publishes every Smartnode's address, and that is the one address source an attacker controlling your peer discovery cannot poison. But the thread that opens those connections waits until the chain is synced, so it is inert during exactly the window where it would break an eclipse.

Looking up history

Any Smartnode can answer, because every Smartnode holds everything and the chain publishes their addresses. A wallet rescanning, an exchange crediting a deposit, an explorer or an auditor asks by position and verifies by hash.

What this buys over deleting history

Kaspa prunes everything past about three days and Grin discards spent outputs permanently. Both are proof of work, both work, and both answer "show me an old transaction" the same way: they cannot, at the protocol level. An archival node there cannot even be synced from the network, only copied from another archive's disk.

Keeping commitments forever means the network always knows exactly what it is missing. Every transaction that ever existed stays nameable from the chain alone. That is the precondition for every retention guarantee, challenge, coverage metric or repair loop — none of which a chain that deleted its own index can ever add.

Two things want reporting and neither needs a method of its own. Body store status, meaning ranges held, disk used and gaps, belongs in the chain info call. Whether a block is body-complete, the new state from section 05, belongs as a field in block output.

A new command is API surface maintained forever. A new field costs nothing to third parties that ignore it.

A third belongs here too: per-peer miss rates and per-range coverage. Section 18 argues for collecting it from day one rather than with the retention layer, and reporting it is a field, not a method.

Mining needs negotiation, not new calls

The template and submit calls both stay. The template needs a rule or capability string, the way segwit used its own marker, and submit has to accept both serialisations. Parameters and rules rather than new methods — and precisely the part external pool software has to be taught, which is why it sits on the schedule risk rather than the engineering one.

10Adversarial surface

Serving history by request is entirely new code. The existing transaction request path never touches disk, so none of its protections transfer.

This section covers the surface the protocol creates. Section 18 is the full threat model, including the attacks that use it — starving a Smartnode's quorum contribution through the same lock that serves bodies, and turning the network's own retry loop into sustained load against the tier.

Amplification

A request entry is 36 bytes and can pull a transaction of up to 1,000,000 bytes — the consensus limit, not the standardness cap — so roughly 28,000 times over. Incoming requests are capped at 50,000 entries per message, so one maximal request can name 50 GB, against up to 125 inbound peers.

The only backpressure is the send buffer, and the disk read happens before the push, so an attacker draining slowly keeps the server seeking. The upload target that bounds block serving is enforced only on the block path and the mempool handler, so body traffic falls outside it entirely.

The honest traffic looks identical

Retention refill produces the same scattered-read profile as the attack. At steady state the network does this to itself, so any defence has to distinguish the two, and identity is the only thing that can. That is another reason the Smartnode tier being identified and addressable from the chain matters more than it first appears.

A privacy regression

Today a peer can only pull a transaction from the mempool if it asked for the mempool first and the transaction predates that request. The rule exists to stop a peer probing whether a node has accepted a given transaction. Serving by identifier from a store written at mempool acceptance bypasses it, and anyone could then test acceptance the instant it happens.

11What a quorum attests to

The proposal was that proof of work mines the block but a Smartnode quorum must approve that the network holds the bodies of every included transaction, and that a block without that approval is orphaned.

Decided — 12 September 2026

The attestation is a record, not a validity gate. Block validity is unchanged. No quorum veto, no halt condition, no censorship surface, no change to chain selection, and no new consensus field. The reasoning is below, and what remains to build is at the end of this section.

What such an attestation can actually carry

A quorum member can only attest that the data exists once it holds the bodies and has assembled the block. But assembling and validating a block is exactly what adopting it as your tip means. So a signature reading "I hold every body in this block" carries the same information as one reading "this is my tip."

That second signature already exists. A ChainLock already means a quorum of Smartnodes each independently validated and adopted that block, which under this design necessarily means each held every body in it. The attestation is not new information. It is the same fact, signed by the same parties, at the same moment.

So the real proposal is make ChainLocks mandatory for block validity rather than advisory. It deserves deciding as that, on its own merits, rather than arriving at sideways through a data-availability argument — because withholding is already defeated by the tip regime, where an unassemblable block never becomes anyone's tip and its producer is never its only possible source.

Option A — the attestation is a gate

A block cannot carry a signature over itself, so the gate cannot be "wait and see whether a ChainLock arrives". That would make validity depend on a timeout, and a timeout is a consensus parameter that nodes with different network views will disagree about. The constructible form is that the attestation rides in the next block: a block is invalid unless it carries a valid quorum signature over its parent. Inspectable, no clock involved, and with precedent in Decred's ticket votes.

What it buys is real: availability becomes an explicit, on-chain, signed fact by identified and collateralised parties, and Smartnodes become a genuine second security tier. What it costs is equally real. If the entire Smartnode set vanished today, Raptoreum would keep producing blocks and only lose ChainLocks and InstantSend. Under Option A the chain halts, because no miner can produce a valid block without a quorum signature over its parent.

Option B — the attestation is a record

The same signature, recorded, with no effect on validity. It buys the one thing an attestation genuinely provides and nothing else here does: identified, collateralised parties on record saying they hold this data. That is what turns retention from a hope into an obligation that can later be enforced, and you cannot penalise a party that never promised anything. It is what Flow's collection guarantee does, and the deferred retention layer will need it either way.

The signing-attempts process is deliberately skipped here and fails when two blocks compete at one height. Choosing the record over the gate means it can no longer stop the chain, which is what that decision bought. But it is the only defence against the single attack that survives the relay rule, so it moves from worth-fixing to required, and before the fetch protocol.

Manufactured quorum splits

This is how the chain behaves today. A quorum member signs only the tip it has connected, and refuses to sign twice at the same height. When two blocks compete at one height the signatures split, no lock forms, and that height falls back to proof-of-work finality. Nothing new.

What decoupling changes is who controls it. Today competing blocks at one height are an accident of near-simultaneous mining: rare, brief, nobody's to schedule. Under decoupling an attacker manufactures the condition by choosing who receives the transaction data and when.

  1. 01

    Deliver selectivelyMine a block, give its data to part of the quorum and withhold from the rest.

  2. 02

    The quorum splits its signaturesThose with the data sign your block. The rest are on the honest sibling and sign that.

  3. 03

    The data then spreads normally, and it does not helpWhoever has it serves it, and gossip repairs availability within seconds. Both halves of the quorum have already spent their signature for that height, and there is no second round.

Why the obvious objection does not hold

"The nodes that have the data will rebroadcast it" is correct and beside the point. The attack never depended on denying data. It depends on the quorum committing signatures before the data finishes spreading, and on the one-signature-per-height rule making that split permanent.

Severity is bounded. No fork and no halt. One height loses near-instant finality and falls back to proof of work, which reopens a double-spend window that locks normally close. It also needs the attacker to catch a race rather than being available on demand.

Why B

Option A is a decision to make Raptoreum a hybrid chain, where proof of work proposes and a collateralised quorum disposes. Coherent and defensible, but not justified by the availability problem, which the tip regime already solves, and it buys a halt condition and a censorship surface that do not exist today.

Option B gets the accountability in full at almost no cost, and the deferred retention layer needs it either way. A contains B, so nothing here forecloses the gate if it is ever wanted.

What still has to be built

"ChainLocks already do this" is nearly true, and falls short in one specific place.

A ChainLock is a recovered threshold signature. By construction it identifies no individual signer, which is the entire point of threshold signatures. So it proves that a quorum adopted the block, and therefore that a quorum held its bodies, but it names nobody who can later be held to it. Accountability needs a party.

The attributable information exists and is thrown away. Each signature share carries the index of the quorum member that produced it, so every contribution is attributable while the signing session runs. Those shares live in memory and are discarded once the recovered signature is assembled.

So the work reduces to retaining attributable attestations, either by persisting the contributing shares alongside the recovered signature, which is cheapest and reuses the existing wire format, or by adding a distinct availability message that members sign and that is kept.

Carry this into the retention design

A ChainLock share asserts "this is my tip", which implies having the bodies, not promising to keep them. Enforcement eventually wants an explicit retention promise, and that is a choice inside option B rather than a settled part of it.

None of it blocks decoupling. It blocks challenges, because a challenge can only penalise a party that promised something, so it should be built before the retention layer rather than before the format work.

13Change ledger

The surprising result of the analysis is how much of the chain is untouched, and how concentrated the new work is.

Unchanged

Works as-is over a commitment block

  • The block header, byte for byte. The merkle root already commits to identifiers and there is no witness variant to reconcile.
  • Proof of work, difficulty, header relay and chain selection by work.
  • Inclusion proofs for light clients, which are built from identifiers alone.
  • Everything below block connection: the transaction loop, script verification, special transactions, the assets subsystem, undo data.
  • ChainLocks and InstantSend, whose lookups all sit inside the recent window.
  • Smartnode registration, which validates collateral through the coins view rather than through history.

New

Has to be designed and built

  • A second block serialization, selected by a negotiated stream flag rather than a header bit, because the version field is both full and hashed.
  • A second "we have it" fact on each block, and the acceptance layer that understands it.
  • The body store: flat files in block order, in a format that can delete records later even though it will not at first.
  • A position-addressed fetch protocol, in which "I do not have that" is a first-class answer from the first day.
  • An incremental accumulator over chain state, plus a canonical record form for asset state, which has none today.
  • Snapshot loading, anchored on a ChainLock over the base rather than on accumulated work, with the transfer bounded before it starts.
  • A relay rule gating forwarding on self-assembly, and a single predicate for whether the bodies are held that every network-triggered path must consult.
  • Passive collection of miss rates and per-range coverage, shipped with the protocol rather than with the retention layer.
Deliberately deferred

Retention mechanics, sharding, challenges, incentives, repair and erasure coding. All of it stays available later precisely because the chain keeps the names. Three decisions must still be taken now, since retrofitting them is a data migration rather than a code change: a deletable store format, a non-punitive answer for a miss, and never pruning commitments.

17The bet

Block acceptance would depend on a network fetch, inside the state machine that decides which chain the node is on.

Several chains split block data from block commitments, and every one of them bought the split by removing the ordering layer's need to validate the data. Flow's consensus nodes work from collection hashes and delegate execution to a different role entirely. Celestia orders and samples and executes nothing. Ethereum's blobs are opaque to the virtual machine. Kaspa and Grin, the two proof-of-work examples, keep validation and delete the data instead.

Here the bodies are the transactions, and nothing can compute the UTXO set without them. That turns availability from a liveness concern into a validity concern, which is why the incomplete state has to exist at all.

Where that leaves it

Three groups exist. Delegate validation away and keep the data: Flow, Celestia, Ethereum. Keep validation and delete the data: Kaspa, Grin. Keep validation, keep the data, and move it somewhere with a guarantee: nobody.

That third line is this design. The Kaspa toolkit supplies the state commitments, the Flow structure supplies an accountable storage tier, and Solana supplies the snapshot-bootstrap model. All three halves have working precedent. Nobody has combined them.

What the security review changed about that

An earlier draft closed by saying everything except the acceptance layer is conventional engineering. That is still true of the difficulty and it was misleading about the risk. Three independent adversarial passes found fourteen attacks, and the cheapest one needs 37 bytes. None of them required a novel technique. They exist because a familiar mechanism was pointed at a new state, and a node that asserts rather than returns an error is a remote crash the moment the data is optional.

So two things should be probed before anything is built on top: the acceptance layer, because it is unprecedented, and every path that can materialise a block, because it is entirely precedented and still fatal. Section 18 lists both.

18Security review

Three independent adversarial passes on a different model, briefed to attack rather than confirm, split across theft, consensus disruption and availability. Plus one on the migration window. Every code claim was verified before recording.

Scope

Pre-existing network defects are out of scope unless decoupling makes them reachable, or unless decoupling cannot ship without them fixed. Several findings below are existing code, listed because this design turns an unreachable assertion into a remote crash, a silent divergence into a permanent fork, or an optional index into a consensus input.

Three rules close most of it

  1. 01

    Never relay a commitment block you cannot assemble yourselfRestores relay atomicity, so a withheld block never propagates as a most-work header. Closes the partition, the mining advantage and the transition split at once.

  2. 02

    Split the non-punitive rule by regimeA miss is normal for history addressed by position. A peer that announced a block keeps today's delivery obligation and today's disconnect. Two reviewers found this independently, and it corrects an earlier recommendation here.

  3. 03

    One predicate for "do I have the bodies"No network-triggered path may materialise a block without consulting it. This closes the entire remote-crash class below.

Plus one that costs almost nothing: record miss rates and per-range coverage passively from day one. Ship the metric with the protocol, not with the retention layer. It is the only way targeted erasure becomes visible before it completes, and it is what the activation criterion needs.

Medium, and the ones not to skip

The InstantSend safety walk fails open, treating a block as safe when it cannot recover its identifier set — which under this design happens exactly when bodies are missing. Losing sibling blocks' bodies become permanent on an append-only store, roughly 26 GB a year from a five percent miner publishing late. And targeted erasure is invisible to consensus, because an attacker running fast Smartnodes that serve everything except one range breaks no rule and the state root does not need that range.

Miss and timeout are different things

The sybil interaction is worse than either half. With part of the Smartnode set answering "not found", and the existing sixty-second wait before another peer is tried, a node that picked a hostile source for a twelve-thousand-transaction block waits a minute per hundred bodies. At a tenth of the set hostile that is one block in ten taking hours, on the critical path to the tip, with the attacker inside the set collecting rewards throughout.

The fix is to stop conflating two answers. A miss is cheap and honest, must never be punished, and should trigger an immediate re-request elsewhere rather than a wait, because the peer already told you it does not have it. A timeout is silence after accepting, and should disconnect exactly as block download does today. Source selection then uses a local throughput score — preference, not punishment, which is what keeps partial retention shippable later.

Downgraded: the fail-open safety walk

Reported as a live hazard, and on examination close to unreachable once the window exists. The walk covers the tip and five blocks below, the identifier sets are held in memory for connected blocks, and the disk fallback runs only after a restart, where the 720-block window guarantees the data is present. The null branch would need a node missing bodies within six blocks of its own tip.

Still worth flipping to fail closed, since it costs nothing and the direction is wrong on principle. Recorded here so it is not re-escalated.

Targeted erasure, in more detail

What the design already gives you. The target stays enumerable forever, so an attacker can make a transaction unanswerable but never unaskable. The erasure produces a permanent, nameable, verifiable hole rather than silence — this position, these Smartnodes asked, none had it. A chain that pruned its identifier lists cannot say that, and for a dispute a hole you can point at is itself evidence.

Measure which ranges, not miss rates. Honest loss and deliberate erasure look nothing alike. Honest loss is oldest-first under disk pressure, or aligned to storage file boundaries, or scattered and small when it is corruption, or a shrinking gap at one end while a node backfills. Deliberate erasure is contiguous, mid-history, aligned to nothing, identical across one operator's nodes, and stable rather than shrinking.

The attacker cannot escape that. To reliably erase a range you must reliably not serve it, and reliable behaviour is what makes a pattern. Randomising to hide means sometimes serving it, which means it survives.

Timing is the whole value. An alarm that fires when replication of a range drops below a threshold is actionable, because someone can re-seed it. One that fires at zero is an obituary. That, rather than monitoring hygiene, is why the coverage metric ships with the protocol.

And it exposes a gap the design never answers: how a new Smartnode obtains history at all. If the answer is fetching through the body protocol, the attacker's fast nodes are the preferred source by construction and the gap propagates to every new Smartnode automatically — the attack rides onboarding rather than fighting attrition. If the answer is copying a datadir, that is the position this design exists to reject, and one corrupt source propagates silently because nothing verifies a store at rest.

The remainder: the retry loop as a peer-steering primitive; a sybil answering "not found" to make one block in ten take hours to assemble; what decoupling adds to a bootstrap eclipse, which is hang-without-disconnect and an unbounded download; opening transaction requests during initial sync; and a reorg past the snapshot base wedging a windowed node.

The migration window

The activation criterion cannot be measured

The cutover is meant to be decided by coverage data. But deployment here votes by block signalling, either hashrate or mined quorum commitments, and neither can see whether anybody holds anything. Coverage itself needs challenges, which are deferred.

So: signal for the cutover with hashrate, old-format blocks stop, and there is no rollback. If coverage was poor, history is gone and nobody could have objected because the measurement never existed. It is also near-unattributable, since it looks like an honest activation that went wrong. The passive coverage metric above is separable from the retention layer and is what this criterion actually requires.

19Build whole, split after

A variant that removes most of the risk in this document, at the cost of the capacity it was originally for. On the analysis below it is the one to build first.

Blocks are constructed, relayed, validated and connected exactly as today, carrying their transactions with them. Each node then splits the validated block locally into its identifier list and its bodies, keeping the list forever and putting the bodies in the store, where they can be pruned and fetched back. The decoupling becomes a storage transformation rather than a change to the wire.

What it removes

The whole acceptance layer, which is section 05 and the one component with no reference implementation anywhere. No second serialisation, no negotiated format flag, no split of have-the-block into two facts, no new validity rung, no durable incomplete state, none of the thirty call sites.

With it goes most of section 18. No withholding, because a block is complete or it does not propagate. No partition of chain selection, no metered-body selfish mining, no manufactured quorum split, no retry-queue poisoning, no assembly-time transaction problem, and no remote crash from a block whose bodies are absent, because the block arrived whole. Mining is untouched, so template and submit handling and every third-party pool implementation carry on unchanged — which deletes the coordination risk that was the schedule threat.

And it may not be a consensus change at all. Splitting a validated block for storage is the same class of local decision as pruning is today: no activation, no dual-format window, no ecosystem deadline, no irreversible cutover.

What it does not give you

Capacity. The block on the wire still carries full transactions, so the size cap still bounds how many fit. The original claim was 32 bytes per transaction inside the block, which is what turns a 2 MB cap into sixty thousand transactions instead of five thousand. This variant keeps storage small and leaves the ceiling exactly where it is.

Getting the capacity means raising the cap, and that is where the honest trade sits. At the tip the wire cost is nearly identical either way, because compact blocks already move short identifiers and the bodies already travelled as ordinary relay. The difference is what a hostile peer can make you download. A 10 MB block of commitments is cheap to reject; a 125 MB block of transactions is not. That is the one genuine argument the commitment format has here, and it only begins to matter at caps far above today's.

One property weakens

In the commitment design the identifier list is the block, so every node necessarily has it and the network provably knows what it is missing. In this variant, keeping the list after pruning the bodies is a local choice. A node could prune everything, and the naming property would hold by convention rather than by construction.

Fixable by making leaves-retained the standard pruning mode, but it moves from guaranteed to conventional, and the naming property is what the whole retention argument rests on.

What it does to the plan

Four of the ten components largely dissolve: the block format, the acceptance layer, mining and migration. What remains is the state root and snapshot sync, the lock and instant-send volume work, the body store, the fetch protocol, and the telemetry. Those were always the parts with known shapes, and two of them already paid for themselves independently.

The sequencing this suggests

Build the storage transformation and the fetch protocol, which are safe and need no fork, and find out whether contract load actually pushes against the block cap.

If it never does, you are finished. If it does, the commitment format is still available, and by then the body store, the fetch protocol and the state root are already running — which were its prerequisites anyway. Nothing is wasted either way.
